# 1. Executive Summary

The National Zoning Reform Act conditions federal infrastructure and transit funding on state and local adoption of zoning reforms that enable housing production near transit and employment centers. This policy addresses America's 3.8-million-unit housing shortage by removing regulatory barriers to construction without requiring new federal appropriations.

**Core Mechanism:** States and localities receiving federal transportation funding must allow:
- Multifamily housing by-right within 0.5 miles of transit
- Accessory dwelling units in all residential zones
- Elimination of parking minimums near transit
- 90-day by-right approval processes

**Anti-Displacement Protections:** Just-cause eviction, tenant right of first refusal, 10-15% inclusionary requirements, relocation assistance.

**Expected Outcomes:**
- 8-15% housing cost reduction in reformed areas
- $20+ billion annual savings for renters
- 0.5-2% GDP growth over 15 years
- 1.2-1.6 million jobs supported

**Political Assessment:** Moderate viability with unusual bipartisan potential. Economists across the spectrum support zoning reform. Recommended vehicle: Infrastructure reauthorization (2026).

# 2. Background & Context

## The Housing Crisis

America faces an acute housing shortage with cascading economic and social consequences:

| Indicator | Current Status | Historical Comparison |
|-----------|----------------|----------------------|
| Housing deficit | 3.8 million units | Largest since WWII |
| Rent burden (>30% income) | 50% of renters | Up from 40% (2000) |
| Median home price/income | 7.5x | 3.5x (1970) |
| Homeownership rate | 65.7% | Down from 69% (2004) |

## Root Causes

**1. Supply Constraints**
Housing production has not kept pace with population and household growth. Annual completions have declined from 2+ million units (1970s) to approximately 1.5 million today, despite population growth.

**2. Restrictive Local Zoning**
Research conclusively demonstrates that local land-use regulations are the primary driver of housing cost variation:

- **Glaeser & Gyourko:** Zoning adds 20-50% to costs in constrained markets
- **Hsieh & Moretti:** Housing constraints cost 36% of potential GDP growth (1964-2009)
- **Single-family-only zones:** 75% of residential land in major metros

**3. Collective Action Failure**
Local governments face incentives to restrict housing:
- Existing homeowners benefit from scarcity (rising home values)
- Costs of exclusion are externalized to the region
- Future residents cannot vote in current zoning decisions
- Fiscal incentives favor low-density development

## Why Federal Intervention

Housing constraints impose costs beyond local boundaries:
- **Reduced national productivity:** Workers cannot relocate to high-productivity areas
- **Increased federal spending:** Housing assistance, homelessness services
- **Infrastructure inefficiency:** Transit investments underutilized without housing
- **Environmental impact:** Sprawl development increases emissions

The federal government has legitimate interest in ensuring its infrastructure investments generate maximum return and that housing markets function efficiently.

# 4. Historical Precedents

## Federal Funding Conditions Precedent

The federal government has successfully used funding conditions to induce state/local policy changes:

| Policy | Year | Mechanism | Outcome |
|--------|------|-----------|---------|
| National Minimum Drinking Age | 1984 | Condition highway funds on age 21 | All states complied by 1988 |
| National Maximum Speed Limit | 1974 | Condition highway funds on 55 mph | All states complied |
| Title VI Civil Rights | 1964 | Condition all federal funds on non-discrimination | Transformed public institutions |
| Clean Air Act | 1970 | Condition highway funds on air quality plans | States adopted SIPs |

**Key principle:** *South Dakota v. Dole* (1987) established that funding conditions are constitutional if:
1. Related to federal spending power
2. Unambiguous conditions
3. Related to federal interest in program
4. Not coercive (reasonable percentage of funds)

## Housing Policy Precedents

- **FHA underwriting:** Historically favored suburban single-family (problematic precedent)
- **HUD fair housing requirements:** Affirmatively Furthering Fair Housing (AFFH) conditions HUD funds on fair housing planning
- **LIHTC QAPs:** States must adopt Qualified Allocation Plans to access tax credits

# 6. Detailed Proposal

## Core Requirements

### 6.1 Transit-Oriented Density

**Requirement:** Jurisdictions must allow multifamily housing by-right within 0.5 miles of fixed transit stations (rail, BRT).

| Transit Type | Minimum Density | Height Limit |
|--------------|-----------------|--------------|
| Heavy rail | 50 units/acre | None required |
| Light rail | 40 units/acre | None required |
| BRT | 30 units/acre | None required |

**By-right:** No discretionary review, conditional use permits, or public hearings for compliant projects. Administrative approval only.

### 6.2 Accessory Dwelling Units

**Requirement:** ADUs permitted by-right in all residential zones.

| Standard | Requirement |
|----------|-------------|
| Size | Up to 1,000 sq ft or 50% of primary |
| Lot coverage | Standard lot coverage applies |
| Parking | No additional parking required |
| Owner occupancy | Not required |
| Design review | Ministerial only |

### 6.3 Parking Reform

**Requirement:** No minimum parking requirements within 0.5 miles of transit.

| Current Problem | Reform |
|-----------------|--------|
| Parking adds 15-20% to construction costs | Eliminate minimums near transit |
| Parking encourages driving | Market-based provision |
| Surface lots waste land near transit | Enable higher density |

### 6.4 Approval Timelines

**Requirement:** 90-day maximum for by-right projects.

| Phase | Maximum Time |
|-------|--------------|
| Completeness determination | 30 days |
| Plan review | 45 days |
| Permit issuance | 15 days |
| **Total** | **90 days** |

Failure to act within 90 days = automatic approval for compliant projects.

## Anti-Displacement Protections

### 6.5 Just-Cause Eviction

**Requirement:** Participating jurisdictions must adopt just-cause eviction protections.

| Permitted Causes | Not Permitted |
|------------------|---------------|
| Non-payment of rent | No-cause evictions |
| Lease violations | Retaliation |
| Owner move-in (limited) | Discrimination |
| Demolition (with relocation) | |

### 6.6 Tenant Right of First Refusal

**Requirement:** When occupied buildings are sold or demolished for redevelopment, existing tenants have:
- 90-day notice of sale
- Right to match purchase offer (for tenant organizations)
- First right to occupy comparable units in new development

### 6.7 Inclusionary Zoning

**Requirement:** New developments over 10 units must include affordable units.

| Development Size | Affordable Requirement | AMI Target |
|------------------|----------------------|------------|
| 10-49 units | 10% | 60% AMI |
| 50-99 units | 12% | 60% AMI |
| 100+ units | 15% | Mix (30-80% AMI) |

In-lieu fee option available for jurisdictions that prefer off-site construction.

### 6.8 Relocation Assistance

**Requirement:** Tenants displaced by demolition or substantial rehabilitation receive:
- 90-day notice
- Relocation payment (3x monthly rent)
- Moving expense reimbursement
- Right to return at comparable rent

# 14. Legal Analysis

## Constitutional Basis

### Spending Clause

Article I, Section 8 grants Congress power to "provide for the ... general Welfare" through spending. *South Dakota v. Dole* (1987) established conditions on spending are constitutional if:

| Test | Application |
|------|-------------|
| General welfare | Housing affordability is national concern ✓ |
| Unambiguous conditions | Clear compliance standards ✓ |
| Related to federal interest | Housing-transit nexus ✓ |
| Not independently unconstitutional | No rights violation ✓ |
| Not coercive | <10% of state budgets ✓ |

### Commerce Clause

Housing markets are interstate commerce. Land-use regulations affecting housing supply have demonstrated effects on labor mobility and economic productivity across state lines.

## Potential Legal Challenges

| Challenge | Risk | Mitigation |
|-----------|------|------------|
| Coercion (*NFIB v. Sebelius*) | Low | Funding at stake is <10% of state budgets |
| 10th Amendment | Low | Conditional spending, not mandate |
| Procedural (APA) | Medium | Robust rulemaking process |
| Equal protection | Low | Facially neutral, rational basis |

## Litigation Strategy

1. Careful DOJ review before rulemaking
2. Extensive administrative record
3. Clear nexus between transit funding and housing
4. Generous phase-in reduces coercion claims
5. State flexibility in implementation

# 16. Arguments For

## Economic Arguments

1. **Removes market distortions:** Local zoning is government regulation restricting housing supply
2. **Improves productivity:** Workers can relocate to high-productivity areas
3. **Creates jobs:** Construction employment, multiplier effects
4. **Fiscally responsible:** No new federal spending required

## Equity Arguments

1. **Addresses exclusionary zoning legacy:** Single-family zoning has racist origins
2. **Benefits lower-income households:** Housing costs burden them disproportionately
3. **Improves economic mobility:** Access to opportunity neighborhoods
4. **Includes displacement protections:** Built-in safeguards

## Environmental Arguments

1. **Reduces sprawl:** Density near transit vs. greenfield development
2. **Lowers emissions:** Less driving, shorter commutes
3. **Maximizes transit investment:** Housing enables ridership
4. **Supported by environmental groups:** Sierra Club, NRDC endorse zoning reform

## Political Arguments

1. **Rare bipartisan consensus:** Economists left and right agree
2. **Proven precedent:** Funding conditions have worked before
3. **State momentum:** Oregon, California, Washington leading
4. **Public support:** Majority supports more housing near transit

# 17. Arguments Against

## Federalism Arguments

1. **Local control tradition:** Zoning has been local prerogative since *Euclid v. Ambler* (1926)
2. **One-size-fits-all concerns:** Different communities have different needs
3. **State preemption preferable:** States, not federal government, should lead

**Response:** Policy preserves state flexibility in implementation; funding conditions are established constitutional practice.

## Implementation Arguments

1. **Complexity:** Defining compliance across 30,000+ jurisdictions
2. **Enforcement challenges:** Monitoring and verification capacity
3. **Political resistance:** Local officials may resist aggressively

**Response:** Clear standards, robust technical assistance, and significant funding at stake create strong incentives.

## Housing Market Arguments

1. **Supply insufficient:** Interest rates and construction costs also constrain
2. **Displacement risk:** New development may raise nearby prices
3. **Gentrification concerns:** Upzoning benefits may not reach lowest-income

**Response:** Anti-displacement protections address concerns; removing regulatory barriers is necessary if not sufficient.

## Political Arguments

1. **NIMBY opposition:** Suburban homeowners resist density
2. **Local official resistance:** Mayors and councils lose discretion
3. **Electoral vulnerability:** Politicians fear angry homeowners

**Response:** Framing, coalition-building, and long phase-in mitigate political risks.

# 19. Alternative Approaches

**Approach:** Increase LIHTC, Housing Choice Vouchers, public housing funding.

| Pro | Con |
|-----|-----|
| Direct affordability impact | Does not address supply constraint |
| Politically tested | Expensive ($10B+ annually) |

**Approach:** Rely on state legislatures to preempt local zoning.

| Pro | Con |
|-----|-----|
| Respects federalism | Uneven adoption |
| Some momentum exists | Slowest states often highest need |

**Approach:** Tax credits for jurisdictions that reform zoning.

| Pro | Con |
|-----|-----|
| Positive incentive | Revenue cost to federal government |
| No penalty perception | Weaker incentive than funding condition |
| Politically easier | May not reach resistant jurisdictions |

**Approach:** Federal government builds housing on federal land.

| Pro | Con |
|-----|-----|
| Bypasses local zoning | Limited federal land in high-cost areas |
| Direct federal action | Does not address broader supply |
| Can target affordability | Small scale relative to deficit |

**Assessment:** Valuable complement, not substitute.

# 20. Equity Considerations

## Historical Context

Single-family zoning emerged in early 20th century explicitly to segregate neighborhoods by race and class. *Buchanan v. Warley* (1917) invalidated explicit racial zoning; localities responded with facially neutral single-family requirements that achieved similar results.

**Legacy Effects:**
- 75% of residential land in major metros zoned single-family only
- Persistent residential segregation
- Wealth gaps from excluded homeownership
- School funding tied to property values

## Equity Analysis of Proposal

| Dimension | Impact |
|-----------|--------|
| **Racial equity** | Reduces exclusionary zoning; opens high-opportunity areas |
| **Income equity** | Benefits lower-income through cost reduction |
| **Geographic equity** | Concentrates on transit areas (varies by access) |
| **Generational equity** | Benefits younger, renter households |

## Potential Equity Concerns

| Concern | Mitigation |
|---------|------------|
| Displacement of existing residents | Just-cause eviction, relocation assistance |
| Gentrification pressures | Inclusionary requirements, right of return |
| Benefits accruing to higher-income | Affordability mandates, AMI targeting |
| Transit access not universal | ADU provisions benefit non-transit areas |

## Equity-Enhancing Amendments

1. Strengthen anti-displacement provisions
2. Target technical assistance to high-poverty jurisdictions
3. Increase inclusionary percentages in high-opportunity areas
4. Fund community land trusts and tenant ownership

# 23. Minority/Dissenting Views

## View 1: Local Control (Conservative Dissent)

**Position:** Housing policy should remain exclusively state and local. Federal conditions on local zoning violate principles of federalism.

**Proponents:** Some traditional conservatives, National League of Cities

**Response:** Funding conditions are established constitutional practice. States remain free to decline federal funds. Local zoning externalities justify federal interest.

## View 2: Insufficient Anti-Displacement (Progressive Dissent)

**Position:** Proposed anti-displacement protections are inadequate. Upzoning without stronger rent stabilization and affordability mandates will accelerate gentrification.

**Proponents:** Some tenant advocacy organizations

**Response:** Provisions represent a floor; states can exceed. Evidence suggests supply expansion moderates prices over time. Stronger provisions may reduce production.

## View 3: Market Solutions Only (Libertarian Dissent)

**Position:** Federal involvement is unnecessary. Simply remove zoning everywhere through state action and let markets work.

**Proponents:** Some libertarian economists

**Response:** State-only action has been too slow. Federal incentives can accelerate. Anti-displacement provisions protect vulnerable residents during transition.

## View 4: Focus on Demand-Side (Alternative Dissent)

**Position:** Supply-side reform is insufficient. Expand housing vouchers and direct assistance instead.

**Proponents:** Some housing advocates, HUD traditionalists

**Response:** Not mutually exclusive. Supply constraints limit effectiveness of demand-side programs. Both are needed.
