# PA-01 Economic Populist: Affordable Housing Policy Proposals

## Topic: Increasing Affordable Housing Availability in Major US Cities

---

## Proposal 1: [BIG IDEA] National Social Housing Development Authority

**Scope:** Big Idea

### Description

Establish a new federal authority—the National Social Housing Development Authority (NSHDA)—capitalized with $500 billion over 10 years to develop, own, and operate permanently affordable mixed-income housing in major metropolitan areas. Unlike traditional public housing or LIHTC (Low-Income Housing Tax Credit) programs that funnel public dollars to private developers and Wall Street investors, this authority would build public wealth while providing housing.

The NSHDA would acquire land (including through eminent domain where necessary to break speculative land banking), construct or rehabilitate buildings, and manage them as publicly-owned assets in perpetuity. Units would be available to households earning up to 150% of Area Median Income, with rents capped at 25% of household income. Critically, units would never convert to market rate—they remain social housing forever. The authority would prioritize union labor for construction, hire locally, and include community benefit agreements requiring 30% local hiring.

This represents a fundamental rejection of the failed model of subsidizing private developers and hoping affordable units trickle down. We've spent decades enriching real estate speculators while working families are priced out of their communities. Social housing exists successfully in Vienna, Singapore, and was pioneered in New York before deliberate disinvestment. It's time to rebuild public capacity to house our people.

### Pros
- **Permanent affordability:** Units remain affordable in perpetuity, unlike LIHTC's 15-30 year compliance periods
- **Builds public wealth:** Assets accrue to the public, not private investors
- **Union jobs:** Mandated project labor agreements create good construction jobs with benefits
- **Anti-speculation:** Removes housing from speculative markets, stabilizing neighborhoods
- **Mixed-income model:** Avoids concentrated poverty, creates sustainable communities

### Cons
- **Massive upfront capital:** Requires significant federal commitment at a time of fiscal constraint
- **Management challenges:** Public housing authorities have mixed track records (though often due to deliberate underfunding)
- **Real estate industry opposition:** Developers, investors, and REITs will fight this vigorously
- **Land acquisition conflicts:** Eminent domain use could generate political backlash
- **Long development timeline:** Housing crisis is immediate; construction takes years

### Implementation Challenges
1. **Congressional approval:** Would require Democratic trifecta and willingness to overcome filibuster for major new spending
2. **Building state/local capacity:** Many housing authorities have been hollowed out and need rebuilding
3. **Supply chain constraints:** Labor and materials shortages could limit construction speed

### Cost Estimate
$500 billion over 10 years in direct federal capital appropriations. Estimated to produce 2-3 million units. Operating costs would be partially offset by rental income from above-median-income tenants. Net federal cost approximately $150,000-200,000 per unit, compared to $250,000+ per LIHTC unit (with no permanent public ownership).

## Proposal 2: [BIG IDEA] Community Land Trust Expansion and Anti-Speculation Tax

**Scope:** Big Idea

### Description

Create a transformational two-part program to permanently remove land from speculative markets and penalize predatory investment practices. First, establish a $50 billion Federal Community Land Trust Fund to capitalize CLTs in every major metropolitan area, enabling community-controlled organizations to acquire land and provide permanent ground leases for affordable housing. Second, implement a federal Anti-Speculation Tax of 25-50% on capital gains from residential property sales where the property was held less than 3 years and the seller is not an owner-occupant.

Community Land Trusts represent the most proven model for permanent affordability. The land is owned by a nonprofit controlled by community members, while homeowners own the building and improvements. When they sell, appreciation is shared between the homeowner and the CLT, keeping the home affordable for the next family forever. Burlington, Vermont's Champlain Housing Trust has operated successfully for 40 years, proving the model works.

The Anti-Speculation Tax directly targets the Wall Street playbook of buying up working-class housing, flipping it for profit, and extracting wealth from communities. Since the 2008 crash, institutional investors like Blackstone have acquired hundreds of thousands of single-family homes, converting them to rentals and driving up prices. Working families competing against private equity with unlimited capital will never win. This tax levels the playing field by making predatory speculation unprofitable.

### Pros
- **Permanent community control:** Land stays in community hands, never returning to speculative market
- **Homeownership pathway:** Working families build wealth while maintaining affordability for next buyer
- **Targets bad actors:** Tax specifically hits speculative investors, not regular homeowners
- **Revenue generation:** Anti-speculation tax generates funds for affordable housing
- **Local democracy:** CLT governance puts residents in control of their neighborhoods

### Cons
- **Slower appreciation for CLT owners:** Shared-equity model limits individual wealth building compared to market-rate ownership
- **Complex governance:** CLTs require active community participation that may be challenging to sustain
- **Tax avoidance strategies:** Sophisticated investors may find loopholes or holding structures to evade tax
- **Constitutional challenges:** Federal speculation tax could face legal challenges under state property rights
- **Market distortions:** Tax could reduce housing liquidity and market efficiency

### Implementation Challenges
1. **Scaling CLT capacity:** Existing CLTs are small; rapid expansion requires significant technical assistance
2. **IRS enforcement:** Distinguishing legitimate investment from predatory speculation requires clear rules
3. **State preemption concerns:** Federal tax may conflict with state tax structures in some jurisdictions

### Cost Estimate
$50 billion Federal CLT Fund over 10 years. Anti-Speculation Tax projected to generate $20-40 billion annually, creating net revenue positive program by year 3. CLT Fund could acquire land for approximately 500,000 permanently affordable homes.

## Proposal 3: Tenant Right to Purchase and First Refusal

**Scope:** Standard

### Description

Require that before any multi-family residential property (5+ units) in federally-assisted areas or opportunity zones can be sold, existing tenants must be given 90 days' right of first refusal to purchase the property at the agreed sale price, either individually or through a tenant association, cooperative, or designated affordable housing nonprofit. Provide federal matching funds of up to $50,000 per unit to assist tenant purchases, administered through a new Tenant Opportunity to Purchase Assistance (TOPA) program.

This policy directly addresses the predatory cycle where working-class tenants see their buildings sold to investors who immediately raise rents, displace longtime residents, and extract wealth from communities. When Blackstone or another private equity firm swoops in to buy an apartment building, tenants have no say and no opportunity. This changes the power dynamic.

Washington, D.C.'s TOPA program has operated since 1980 and has enabled thousands of tenants to purchase their buildings and maintain affordability. San Francisco and other cities have similar programs. Federal expansion would create a universal floor while allowing stronger state/local programs to remain in place.

### Pros
- **Tenant empowerment:** Gives working-class renters agency over their housing fate
- **Wealth building:** Cooperative ownership allows tenants to build equity
- **Prevents displacement:** Keeps longtime residents in their communities
- **Proven model:** D.C. and San Francisco programs demonstrate feasibility
- **Market-based:** Works within existing property markets rather than replacing them

### Cons
- **Slows transactions:** 90-day waiting period could frustrate some legitimate sales
- **Financing challenges:** Tenant groups often struggle to secure loans even with assistance
- **Technical complexity:** Forming cooperatives and conducting due diligence requires expertise tenants may lack
- **Limited reach:** Only applies to sales, not to rent increases or other displacement tactics
- **Seller resistance:** Property owners may structure deals to evade requirements

### Implementation Challenges
1. **Technical assistance infrastructure:** Need network of nonprofit advisors to help tenants exercise rights
2. **Financing pipeline:** CDFIs and credit unions need capacity to underwrite cooperative purchases
3. **Enforcement mechanisms:** Clear penalties needed for sellers who evade notice requirements

### Cost Estimate
$10 billion over 5 years for TOPA matching fund program. Estimated to enable tenant purchase of 75,000-100,000 units. Administrative costs approximately $200 million for technical assistance grants to nonprofits.

## Proposal 4: Zoning Reform with Anti-Displacement Protections

**Scope:** Standard

### Description

Condition federal transportation and community development funding on states and localities adopting zoning reforms that allow multi-family housing near transit and job centers, while requiring concurrent anti-displacement protections. Specifically, jurisdictions must: (1) eliminate single-family-only zoning within 1 mile of transit stations; (2) allow accessory dwelling units by-right; (3) reduce minimum parking requirements; AND (4) enact just-cause eviction protections, rent stabilization for existing units, and inclusionary zoning of 20% affordable units for new development over 10 units.

The YIMBY movement is correct that restrictive zoning creates artificial scarcity that drives up housing costs. However, simply allowing more market-rate development without protections will accelerate gentrification and displacement of working-class residents. We've seen this play out in cities that upzoned without safeguards—longtime residents pushed out as "revitalization" benefits newcomers.

This policy threads the needle: increase supply while protecting existing residents. The federal funding condition creates powerful incentive without mandates that could face constitutional challenges. States and localities choose whether to access funding, but cannot take federal dollars while perpetuating exclusionary housing policies.

### Pros
- **Addresses supply constraints:** Enables housing construction where demand is highest
- **Protects existing residents:** Anti-displacement requirements prevent zoning reform from becoming gentrification engine
- **Uses existing leverage:** Conditions funding rather than creating new programs
- **Climate co-benefits:** Transit-oriented density reduces car dependence and emissions
- **Bipartisan appeal:** Supply-focused reforms have support across political spectrum when paired with protections

### Cons
- **Local resistance:** Suburban homeowners and some existing residents oppose density
- **Implementation variation:** Protections will be stronger in some jurisdictions than others
- **Development friction:** Inclusionary requirements may reduce overall construction in some markets
- **Enforcement complexity:** Monitoring compliance across thousands of jurisdictions is challenging
- **Displacement during transition:** Even with protections, some displacement may occur during upzoning process

### Implementation Challenges
1. **Defining compliance:** Clear metrics needed for what constitutes sufficient anti-displacement protections
2. **State preemption issues:** Some states have preempted local rent stabilization, creating compliance barriers
3. **Political feasibility:** Tying transportation funding to housing could face resistance from transportation committees

### Cost Estimate
No direct federal appropriation required—works through existing funding streams (CDBG, transportation grants). Estimated $5-10 billion in current federal funds could be conditioned. Administrative costs approximately $50 million annually for compliance monitoring and technical assistance.

## Proposal 5: Public Banking for Affordable Housing Finance

**Scope:** Standard

### Description

Create a federal charter framework for public banks at the state and municipal level with a mandate to provide below-market financing for affordable housing development and preservation. Provide $20 billion in federal seed capital for qualifying public banks and require that they maintain at least 50% of their loan portfolio in affordable housing, community land trusts, limited-equity cooperatives, and nonprofit developers. Prohibit public banks from financing luxury development or speculative investment purchases.

The current housing finance system is rigged against working families. Private banks prefer financing luxury condos and market-rate apartments because the returns are higher. Community development financial institutions (CDFIs) do important work but lack the scale to meet demand. Meanwhile, billions in public deposits sit in Wall Street banks that use them to finance the very speculation driving the housing crisis.

Public banking for housing follows successful models in North Dakota (Bank of North Dakota since 1919) and emerging programs in California and other states. By capturing the spread between deposit rates and lending rates for public benefit rather than private profit, public banks can offer financing at 1-2 percentage points below market, making affordable projects pencil out that otherwise wouldn't.

### Pros
- **Below-market financing:** Enables projects that can't compete for private capital
- **Patient capital:** Public banks can accept longer timelines and lower returns than private lenders
- **Recycles public deposits:** Uses government funds productively rather than enriching Wall Street
- **Local accountability:** Governed by public officials responsive to community needs
- **Counter-cyclical capacity:** Can continue lending during economic downturns when private capital retreats

### Cons
- **Political risk:** Changes in administration could undermine bank mission or management
- **Management capacity:** Finding qualified bankers willing to work at public-sector salaries
- **Competition concerns:** Private banks will lobby against public competition
- **Risk concentration:** Heavy affordable housing focus creates portfolio concentration risk
- **Start-up challenges:** Building lending infrastructure and expertise takes time

### Implementation Challenges
1. **State enabling legislation:** Many states lack legal framework for public banks, requiring legislative action
2. **FDIC coordination:** Federal deposit insurance framework wasn't designed for public banks
3. **Capitalization timeline:** Building sufficient capital base takes years before meaningful lending capacity

### Cost Estimate
$20 billion in federal seed capital over 5 years, deployed as equity contributions to qualifying public banks. With leverage, could support $100-150 billion in affordable housing lending over 10 years. Ongoing costs limited to federal regulatory oversight, approximately $25 million annually.

| Proposal | Scope | Cost (10yr) | Units/Impact |

These proposals share a common thread: shifting power from Wall Street, private equity, and real estate speculators to working families and their communities. Housing should be a human right and a foundation for stable communities, not an asset class for extracting wealth from those who can least afford it.
