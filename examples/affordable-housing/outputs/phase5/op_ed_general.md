# Opinion: It's Time for National Zoning Reform

**Policy Council Phase 5 | Op-Ed (General Audience)**

*Suggested placement: Major national newspaper opinion section*
*Word count: ~800 words*

---

When a teacher can't afford to live in the city where she teaches, something is broken. When a nurse commutes two hours each way because housing near the hospital costs more than her paycheck, the system has failed. When young families are priced out of the communities where they grew up, we're not just facing a housing problem — we're watching the American Dream slip away.

America is nearly 4 million homes short of what we need. In cities like San Francisco, Boston, and Seattle, housing costs have become so extreme that middle-class professionals qualify for housing assistance. Nationally, half of all renters spend more than 30 percent of their income on housing.

How did we get here? The answer might surprise you: government regulation.

### The Zoning Problem

In nearly every American city, local zoning laws dictate exactly what can be built on private land. These rules — often written decades ago — prohibit apartments in most neighborhoods, require expensive parking spaces, and create bureaucratic hurdles that add years to construction timelines.

The research is overwhelming. Economists at Harvard, Stanford, and Chicago have documented that zoning restrictions add 20 to 50 percent to housing costs in high-demand areas. One landmark study found that housing constraints in a handful of productive cities reduced American GDP growth by more than a third over 45 years.

The problem isn't that individual homeowners are greedy. It's that our system creates perverse incentives. Existing homeowners benefit when housing is scarce — their property values rise. Future residents can't vote in today's zoning decisions. And the costs of exclusion get pushed onto the region while benefits stay local.

This is a collective action problem that no single city can solve. Which is why we need federal leadership.

### A Solution That Both Parties Can Support

The National Zoning Reform Act, recently introduced in Congress, offers a path forward. The bill conditions federal transportation funding on local adoption of commonsense zoning reforms: allowing apartments near transit, legalizing backyard cottages, eliminating parking mandates near train stations, and speeding up approvals.

This isn't a federal takeover of local planning. It's a reasonable condition on federal investment — just like we've done for decades with highway safety and environmental standards. If a city wants federal transit dollars, it makes sense to require housing near that transit. Otherwise, we're investing in trains that people can't afford to ride.

What makes this proposal different is its bipartisan appeal. Conservatives can embrace it as deregulation — local zoning is government regulation restricting what property owners can build on their own land. Progressives can champion it as housing justice — exclusionary zoning has roots in racial segregation and continues to limit economic mobility for lower-income families.

The bill requires no new federal spending. It restructures existing funding streams. And it includes strong protections against displacement: just-cause eviction requirements, tenant rights of first refusal, and affordability mandates for new construction.

### Learning From Success

We're not guessing that this approach works. Oregon, California, and Washington have already passed state-level zoning reforms. Minneapolis eliminated single-family-only zoning and saw rents moderate compared to similar cities. Auckland, New Zealand, implemented comprehensive reform and saw rents fall 26-33 percent below what they would have been.

Japan offers the most striking example. With a national zoning system rather than thousands of local codes, Tokyo permits 130,000 new homes each year — four times as many as New York. Japanese housing prices have remained stable for decades while American cities have become unaffordable.

### The Path Forward

The infrastructure reauthorization bill coming due in 2026 offers the perfect vehicle. Transportation and housing are intimately connected — you can't have functioning transit without housing nearby, and you can't have affordable housing without access to jobs.

This isn't about choosing between homeowners and renters, or between existing residents and newcomers. It's about building enough housing that everyone can afford a place to live. Research shows that when we build more housing, prices moderate — benefiting renters and stabilizing neighborhoods.

The policy won't solve everything. Interest rates, construction costs, and labor shortages all matter too. But removing regulatory barriers is essential. When conditions improve, reformed areas will be ready to build. Cities that cling to exclusionary zoning will be left behind.

### A Rare Opportunity

In an era of partisan gridlock, housing affordability is one issue where evidence points clearly to solutions that both sides can support. Economists from the Cato Institute and the Brookings Institution agree. The YIMBY movement has chapters in red states and blue states. Business groups and civil rights organizations are on the same side.

The question is whether we'll seize this opportunity or continue watching as housing costs consume ever-larger shares of American paychecks.

The teacher, the nurse, the young family — they're waiting for us to act.
