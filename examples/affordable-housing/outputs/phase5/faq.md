# Frequently Asked Questions
## National Zoning Reform Act

**Policy Council Phase 5 | FAQ Document**

---

## General Questions

### What is the National Zoning Reform Act?

The National Zoning Reform Act conditions federal infrastructure and transit funding on state and local adoption of zoning reforms that allow more housing production, particularly near transit and employment centers.

If localities want federal transit dollars, they must allow housing near transit.

### Why is this needed?

America faces a shortage of nearly 4 million housing units. Research shows that restrictive local zoning is the primary driver of housing cost variation across cities, adding 20-50% to costs in high-demand markets.

Local governments have incentives to restrict housing: existing homeowners benefit from scarcity, and future residents can't vote in current zoning decisions. This creates a collective action problem that individual cities cannot solve.

### How does this work?

The bill conditions receipt of federal transportation funding on compliance with "Housing Opportunity Standards." These standards require:

- Allowing multifamily housing near transit stations
- Legalizing accessory dwelling units (backyard cottages)
- Eliminating parking minimums near transit
- Streamlining approval processes to 90 days

Localities that don't comply gradually lose access to federal transportation funding.

### Does this force cities to change their zoning?

No. Cities remain free to decline federal funding. This is a condition on federal spending, not a mandate.

However, because significant transportation funding is at stake, most cities will have strong incentives to comply.

## Policy Details

### What are the specific requirements?

**Transit-Oriented Density:**
- Multifamily housing permitted by-right within 0.5 miles of transit
- Minimum densities: 30-50 units per acre depending on transit type
- No discretionary reviews for compliant projects

**Accessory Dwelling Units:**
- Allowed by-right in all residential zones
- No owner-occupancy requirement
- No additional parking required
- Minimum 800 sq ft or 50% of primary dwelling

**Parking Reform:**
- No minimum parking requirements within 0.5 miles of transit
- Shared parking permitted

**Approval Timelines:**
- 90-day maximum for qualifying projects
- Automatic approval if timeline missed

### What anti-displacement protections are included?

The bill requires participating jurisdictions to adopt:

- **Just-cause eviction:** Tenants can only be evicted for specified reasons (non-payment, lease violations, owner move-in, etc.)
- **Tenant right of first refusal:** When multi-unit buildings are sold, tenants can match purchase offers
- **Inclusionary zoning:** 10-15% of new units must be affordable
- **Relocation assistance:** Displaced tenants receive 3 months rent plus moving costs

### What is the timeline?

| Year | Requirement |
|------|-------------|
| 1 | Rulemaking, define standards |
| 2-3 | Pilot program (5% bonus) |
| 4 | Compliance plans due |
| 5 | Progress demonstrations |
| 6 | Full compliance for discretionary grants |
| 7+ | Formula fund conditions apply |

## Legal Questions

### Is this constitutional?

Yes. The Supreme Court has repeatedly upheld federal funding conditions under the Spending Clause. Key precedents:

- **South Dakota v. Dole (1987):** Upheld conditioning highway funds on drinking age
- **National speed limits:** Conditioned highway funds on 55 mph limit
- **Title VI:** Conditions all federal funds on non-discrimination

The *Dole* test requires that conditions be: (1) related to spending power, (2) unambiguous, (3) related to federal interest, and (4) not coercive. This bill meets all tests.

### Could this be challenged in court?

Any significant legislation may face legal challenges. However:

- The constitutional basis is strong
- Precedent clearly supports funding conditions
- The funding at stake is well below coercion thresholds
- Generous phase-in reduces claims of unfairness

DOJ review is recommended before implementation.

### Doesn't this violate local control?

No. Local governments retain full authority over their zoning codes. They simply must meet minimum standards to receive federal funding.

This is the same principle as highway safety standards, environmental requirements, and civil rights non-discrimination. The federal government regularly conditions funding on policy goals.

## Cost and Impact

### How much does this cost?

**Federal costs:** Approximately $120 million per year for:
- Administrative implementation ($15-25M)
- Technical assistance ($100M)

**New appropriations:** $0. The bill restructures existing funding.

**State/local costs:** $100K-$800K one-time per jurisdiction for code updates and staff training.

### What are the expected benefits?

| Benefit | Estimate |
|---------|----------|
| Annual renter savings | $20+ billion |
| GDP gains (15 years) | $130-520 billion |
| Jobs supported | 1.2-1.6 million/year |
| Benefit-cost ratio | 600-950:1 |

### Will this actually lower housing costs?

Evidence suggests yes. Studies find:

- Auckland, NZ saw rents 26-33% lower after comprehensive reform
- Minneapolis saw rents 16-34% lower than comparison cities
- Tokyo produces 4x as many homes as NYC with stable prices

The magnitude depends on adoption rates and market conditions, but the direction is clear.

### Who benefits?

- **Renters:** Direct cost reduction
- **First-time buyers:** More inventory, lower prices
- **Workers:** Access to high-productivity labor markets
- **Lower-income households:** Spend highest share on housing
- **Construction workers:** More jobs

### Who might be affected negatively?

- **Existing homeowners:** May see slower appreciation (2-5% reduction in appreciation rates)
- **Some local officials:** Lose discretionary authority

## Political Questions

### Is this partisan?

No. The bill has unusual bipartisan potential because:

- **Conservatives** can frame it as deregulation and property rights
- **Progressives** can emphasize equity and anti-displacement protections
- **Economists across the spectrum** agree that zoning reform is needed

### Who supports it?

**Organizations:**
- YIMBY Alliance
- National Association of Realtors
- Tech companies (workforce housing)
- Environmental groups (density reduces emissions)

**Think tanks:**
- Brookings Institution
- Cato Institute
- American Enterprise Institute
- Urban Institute

### Who opposes it?

- **National League of Cities:** Local control concerns
- **Some homeowner associations:** Neighborhood change concerns
- **Some tenant advocates:** Want stronger anti-displacement provisions

## Implementation Questions

### How will compliance be monitored?

1. **Self-certification:** Jurisdictions submit annual compliance reports
2. **Random audits:** HUD audits 20%+ of certifications annually
3. **Third-party challenges:** Anyone can challenge a certification
4. **Appeals process:** Administrative hearings before funding reduction

### What help is available for localities?

$100 million annually for technical assistance:
- Model ordinance development
- Planning capacity grants
- Staff training
- Compliance monitoring tools

Small and capacity-limited jurisdictions receive priority.

### What if my city can't comply in time?

The generous timeline (5-7 years) provides ample time for code updates. Cities that need more help can:

- Apply for technical assistance grants
- Use model ordinances
- Request early compliance review

### What about historic districts?

Limited exemptions may be available for designated historic districts established before the bill's enactment, provided alternative density is allowed elsewhere.

## Common Objections

### "This is a federal takeover of local planning"

It's not. Local governments retain full authority over their zoning codes. The bill simply conditions one funding stream on meeting minimum housing standards — the same approach used for highway safety, environmental protection, and civil rights.

### "Neighborhoods will be destroyed"

The requirements apply primarily to areas near transit. Single-family neighborhoods away from transit are largely unaffected. And neighborhoods change over time regardless — the question is whether change is planned or chaotic.

### "This will cause gentrification and displacement"

The bill includes the strongest anti-displacement protections in any federal housing legislation. And evidence shows that building more housing actually reduces displacement pressure by moderating prices.

### "Construction won't happen anyway due to costs"

Removing regulatory barriers is necessary even if not sufficient. When interest rates fall or construction costs moderate, reformed areas will be ready to build. The policy creates "option value."

### "One size doesn't fit all"

The standards are flexible — jurisdictions can exceed them and have discretion in implementation details. The bill sets floors, not ceilings.
